                Start

                The 60-second version

                If you read nothing else, do these seven things. The rest of the guide shows you how.

                1. Make the first scan easy.Recruiters glance at job titles, employers, dates and numbers first. Use clear headings, one column and plenty of white space.
                2. Write achievements, not duties.Every bullet should say what you did and what changed because of it, with a number where you can.
                3. Tailor it to every job.Mirror the job ad's title and key terms where they're true for you. Keep a master resume and trim a copy for each application.
                4. Leave off personal details.No photo, date of birth, marital status or street address. Suburb, phone and a professional email are enough.
                5. Put licences and tickets where they're seen.For many jobs, an RSA, White Card, forklift licence or Working With Children Check decides who gets a call.
                6. Sound like a person.Use AI to tighten your writing if you like, but replace generic phrases with specifics only you could write.
                7. Check it like a stranger would.Read it aloud, test that the text copies cleanly, and ask someone else to proofread.

                Start

                How resumes are really read

                Your resume has one job: to get you an interview. To do that, it has to get through a fast skim and then hold up to a careful read.

                ~7 sec

                The first scan

                In an eye-tracking study, recruiters spent an average of 7.4 seconds on the initial screen, looking hardest at job titles. Simple layouts with clear headings did best.1

                Minutes

                The careful read

                Resumes that pass the scan get read properly, often more than once, and again before the interview. This is where your evidence and specifics count.

                Search

                The software

                Most applications go into an applicant tracking system (ATS). Recruiters search and sort in it, so the job's key terms need to be in your resume as plain text.

                More applicants means a faster skim

                When a job attracts hundreds of applications, the first pass has to be quick. A resume that makes your fit obvious in seconds gets the careful read.

                The robots rarely reject you

                The claim that 75% of resumes are rejected by software before a person sees them has no solid source. In one survey of 25 recruiters, 92% said their systems don't auto-reject for formatting, design or missing keywords. The real automatic filters are knock-out questions such as work rights, licences, minimum qualifications and location.3 Answer those carefully and truthfully.

                AI has made resumes look alike

                92% of recruiting leaders say AI-written resumes are now commonplace, and only a third of employers are highly confident resumes reflect real skills.5 Specific, checkable details are what stand out now.

                Write

                The Australian basics

                Australians use "resume" and "CV" almost interchangeably; a CV usually means a longer document for academic or research roles. The standard layout is reverse-chronological, with your most recent job first.7

                How long should it be?
                Your stageTypical lengthWhat fills it

                Referees: list them, or "available on request"?

                Both are common in Australia. Employers usually only contact referees once they have a preferred candidate, so SEEK suggests you consider leaving their details off your resume.13 If the ad asks for referees, include them.

                Either way, get permission first and have two or three ready, ideally recent supervisors.14

                Write it the Australian way

                • Australian spelling: organised, prioritised, colour, centre.
                • Dates as month and year: "Mar 2023 – Present".
                • Local terms: "Year 12" and "TAFE", with overseas qualifications explained.
                • Licences and tickets by their local names, such as RSA, White Card and WWCC.

                Write

                Section by section

                Use these sections in this order. Leave out any that don't help you, and move licences higher if they're essential for the job.

                1. Contact details

                  Name in large type, then suburb and state, mobile, email and an optional LinkedIn link. Keep them in the body of the page, not in a header or footer.

                  ExamplePriya Sharma · Geelong VIC · 0400 000 000 · priya.sharma@email.com

                2. Headline

                  A short line saying what you are, ideally echoing the job title in the ad, if it's honest.

                  ExampleDisability Support Worker · NDIS Worker Screening Check · Manual driver's licence

                3. Summary

                  Three or four lines on what you offer this employer: experience, strongest skills and one proof point. Skip the old "career objective", which talks about what you want.7

                  ExampleSupport worker with four years in community and residential care. Calm in complex behaviours, trained in medication assistance, and known for building trust with clients and families.

                4. Key skills

                  Eight to twelve skills that match the job ad, grouped if you like. Each one should be backed up somewhere in your experience.

                  ExamplePersonal care · Medication assistance · Positive behaviour support · Shift handovers · ShiftCare and Microsoft 365

                5. Work experience

                  Most recent first. For each job: title, employer, location and dates, a one-line description if the business isn't well known, then three to six achievement bullets.

                  ExampleSupport Worker · Bayside Care Services, Geelong · Feb 2022 – Present
                  • Supported 6 clients a week with personal care and community access, with no missed shifts in 2025

                6. Education and training

                  Highest or most relevant first: qualification, institution and year. Include a high school only if it's your highest level or you've just left.

                  ExampleCertificate IV in Disability Support · The Gordon TAFE · 2021

                7. Licences, tickets and checks

                  A dedicated section employers can spot instantly: driver's licence class, White Card, RSA, RCG, First Aid and CPR, forklift licence, Working With Children Check, NDIS Worker Screening, police check. Add expiry dates where relevant.

                  ExampleFirst Aid and CPR (current to Aug 2027) · Working With Children Check (VIC) · NDIS Worker Screening Check

                8. Volunteering, projects or interests

                  Worth including when they show relevant skills, especially for a first job or a career change.

                  ExampleVolunteer coach, Geelong Junior Basketball, 2019 – present

                9. Referees

                  Names, roles, relationship and contact details, or "Available on request".

                  ExampleMark Ellis · Team Leader, Bayside Care Services (my supervisor) · 0400 000 001

                Write achievements, not duties

                Duties describe the job. Achievements describe you. Anyone with your title had the same duties; what the employer wants to know is how well you did them. Even government recruiters, such as ASIC's, ask candidates to quantify achievements where possible.15

                The formula: action verb + what you did + the result, with a number where possible.

                    Write

                    Tailor it to the job

                    Keep one master resume with every job, achievement and course you've ever done.16 For each application, copy it and spend 15 to 20 minutes making it fit:

                    1. Headline: match the ad's job title, if it's honest.
                    2. Summary: lead with what this employer cares about most.
                    3. Skills: reorder so the ad's must-haves come first.
                    4. Bullets: move your most relevant achievements to the top of each job, and cut the least relevant.
                    5. Language: use the ad's words for things you genuinely do. If they say "client", don't say "customer".

                    Polish

                    Format it for people and software

                    A plain, well-spaced layout helps both audiences. Recruiters found cluttered resumes with multiple columns and long sentences harder to read,1 and applicant tracking systems often scramble columns, tables, text boxes and icons when they pull your details into their database.4

                    Do

                    • One column, with standard headings like "Work experience" and "Education".
                    • A common font such as Calibri, Arial or Aptos at 10 to 12 point.
                    • Bold job titles and consistent dates so the eye can run down the page.
                    • Short bullets of one or two lines.
                    • A text-based PDF or Word file, whichever the ad asks for.
                    • A clear file name: Priya-Sharma-Resume.pdf

                    Don't

                    • Two-column templates, tables or text boxes for key information.
                    • Skill bars, star ratings, icons or charts.
                    • Contact details hidden in the header or footer.
                    • Scanned or photographed resumes; software can't read them.
                    • Keywords hidden in white text. Parsers extract it and recruiters treat it as a red flag.4
                    • Heavy colour, photos or unusual fonts.15

                    The 10-second test: open your PDF, select all the text, copy it and paste it into a plain email. If it comes out in a sensible order, software can read it too. If it's jumbled or missing, simplify the layout.

                    Polish

                    Using AI without sounding like everyone else

                    AI is now part of most job searches. In a 2026 survey of US hiring managers, 77% said many resumes they see look AI-generated, and while most said overall quality had gone up, resumes have also become more uniform.6 The fix is specifics.

                    good uses

                    Let AI help with

                    • Turning a list of duties into achievement bullets you then check.
                    • Spotting the key requirements in a job ad.
                    • Tightening long sentences and fixing grammar.
                    • Suggesting interview questions your resume might raise.
                    watch out

                    Don't let AI

                    • Invent numbers, skills or responsibilities. You'll be asked about them.
                    • Fill your resume with phrases like "results-driven professional" or "proven track record".
                    • Write in a voice you wouldn't use in an interview.
                    • See more personal data than it needs. Remove your contact details before pasting your resume into an AI tool.

                    Polish

                    Advice for your situation

                    First job or school leaver

                    One page. Lead with a short summary, your availability and any licences. Use school, sport, volunteering and casual work as evidence of reliability, learning and dealing with people.

                    Graduate

                    Put your degree near the top. Treat major projects, placements and part-time jobs like work experience, with achievement bullets. Graduate programs often screen on these first.

                    Changing careers

                    Open with a summary that names the new target role. Group transferable skills near the top, retitle bullets around the new field's priorities, and add recent study or short courses. A cover letter helps here.

                    Returning after a break

                    Show the gap honestly as a line such as "Career break: full-time carer, 2023 – 2025", plus anything you did to stay current. Half of hiring managers in a LinkedIn survey said people returning from a break often gain valuable soft skills.19

                    Over 50

                    Age bias is real: nearly one in five Australian HR professionals said they were reluctant, or only willing to a limited extent, to recruit people aged 50 to 64.18 Detail the last 10 to 15 years, leave off graduation years, and show current tools and recent training.

                    Overseas experience

                    State your work rights or visa clearly. Add a few words about overseas employers ("national bank, 4,000 staff") and give Australian equivalents for qualifications where you can.

                    Trades, mining and FIFO

                    Put tickets, licences and machinery or equipment near the top. Mention safety records, site types and rosters you've worked, such as 2:1 or 8:6.

                    Government jobs

                    Follow the job pack exactly. Many agencies want a one or two page pitch or responses to selection criteria using the STAR model, alongside your resume.20

                    Polish

                    Stay honest, and stay safe

                    Don't stretch the truth

                    Lying on resumes is common: in a 2025 US survey, 24% of job seekers admitted it, most often about years of experience, skills and how long they held a job.17 It's also easy to catch. Reference checks, qualification checks, licence registers and interview questions all test what you claim, and a discovered lie can cost you the job even after you've started.

                    Present yourself in the best honest light instead: round sensibly, describe your real contribution to team results, and explain gaps briefly.

                    Watch for job scams

                    Australians reported losing about $18.5 million to job and employment scams in 2025.12 Scammers post fake ads to collect bank details, tax file numbers and passport details, then use them for identity fraud.11

                    • Keep ID numbers, your TFN and bank details off your resume.
                    • Be wary of jobs that need upfront payment or offer high pay for little work.
                    • Check the employer using contact details from its official website.
                    • Report scams at scamwatch.gov.au.

                    Use whatever the job ad asks for. Otherwise, a text-based PDF keeps your layout intact. Check that you can select and copy the text in it; a scanned or image PDF can't be read by screening software.

                    Should I put a photo on my resume?

                    No. Australian employers don't expect a photo, and it invites bias. The exception is work where appearance is part of the job, such as acting or modelling.

                    How far back should my history go?

                    Cover the last 10 to 15 years in detail. Older roles can go in a short "Earlier career" line with titles and employers only, unless they're highly relevant.

                    Do I include my full address?

                    No. Your suburb and state show you're local. Leaving out your street address protects your privacy if your resume is shared or ends up with a scammer.

                    Resume or CV?

                    Most Australians use the words interchangeably. A CV usually means a longer document for academic, research or medical roles, listing publications, grants and teaching.

                    Do I need a different resume for every job?

                    Yes, but it doesn't take long. Keep one master resume, then spend 15 to 20 minutes tailoring a copy for each job: the title, summary, skills order and the bullets you lead with.

                    Should I include hobbies?

                    Only if they show something relevant, such as a team sport for a first job, coaching for a leadership role, or a hobby that uses the job's skills. Otherwise use the space for evidence.

                    Start

                    Do you need a cover letter?

                    The research disagrees. In a 2023 survey of 625 US hiring managers, 83% said they always or frequently read cover letters, and 45% read the letter before the resume.1 Another US survey in 2024 found that 44% of hiring managers never read them.2 Habits vary by person, company and role, and you won't know which reader you'll get.

                    What readers want from it

                    Hiring managers said the main reason they read cover letters is for context about why you're applying. Small companies mostly use them to judge fit for the role.1

                    The opening matters most

                    The introduction left the biggest impression of any part of the letter (41%), and 49% said a strong letter can earn an interview for a candidate whose resume is weaker.1

                    Our rule of thumb

                    Write one unless the ad tells you not to, or the application form gives you nowhere to put it. Australian employer advice is the same: include one unless you're asked not to.7

                    Always send one when

                    • The ad asks for one, or for a "statement" or "pitch".
                    • You're changing careers and the resume alone won't make the link.
                    • You have a gap, are relocating, or are stepping back to a more junior role.
                    • You're applying to a small business, where the owner often reads everything.
                    • You're writing to an employer with no job advertised, as an expression of interest.

                    It's fine to skip it when

                    • The ad or the recruiter says not to send one.
                    • The online form has no upload or text box for it.
                    • A recruiter is submitting you; send them a short note instead.
                    • You'd only be sending a generic letter. No letter beats a bad one.

                    Start

                    What AI has changed

                    A cover letter used to signal effort: a tailored letter took time, so it said something about how much you wanted the job. AI has made polished letters free, and the research shows what happened next.

                    Better letters, not more interviews

                    In a Tilburg University experiment, letters written with ChatGPT scored higher with recruiters from companies such as PwC and Philips, but didn't lead to more interview invitations. AI improved the standard parts; the motivation stayed much the same.3

                    Tailoring stopped standing out

                    After a freelance platform launched an AI cover letter writer, the link between how tailored a letter looked and whether it got a callback fell by 51%.4

                    Employers stopped paying for polish

                    Before AI, employers on the same platform paid more for workers whose applications were tailored to the job. After AI arrived, that premium disappeared.5

                    What this means for you: smooth wording no longer impresses anyone. What still stands out is what AI can't invent: your real results, specific knowledge of this employer, and a believable reason for wanting the job. Use AI to tidy your draft if you like, but the facts and the reasons have to come from you.

                      ExampleYour ad asks for someone who can coach a team of six. I've trained six new starters over the past year, and all of them passed probation.

                    Frequently asked questions

                    Do I need one if it's optional?

                    Usually, yes. Some hiring managers never read them and others always do, and you can't tell which you'll get. A short, specific letter costs you 20 minutes and can only help, as long as it's tailored.

                    How long should it be?

                    Aim for 250 to 400 words in three to five short paragraphs, on less than one page. Most HR professionals prefer one page or less.

                    Who do I address it to?

                    Use the hiring manager's name if the ad, the company website or LinkedIn gives it. Otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager" is the safest generic greeting. Avoid "To whom it may concern" and "Dear Sir/Madam".

                            Frequently asked questions

                            Do I need LinkedIn to get a job?

                            Not for every job, but it helps for most office, professional, government, tech, health and management roles, and for jobs found through recruiters. Even in hands-on work, larger employers often look candidates up. A simple, complete profile is enough.

                            Do I need Premium?

                            No. Recruiters using LinkedIn's recruiting tools can search all members. A complete profile with the right title, skills and location matters far more than a paid subscription.

                            Can my employer see I'm open to work?

                            If you choose "Recruiters only", LinkedIn takes steps to hide your status from recruiters at your current company, but it can't guarantee complete privacy. The public green frame is visible to everyone, including your colleagues.

                            Can I use an AI-edited headshot?

                            Light editing is fine, but the photo must look like you. LinkedIn requires a profile photo to reflect your real likeness, and an interviewer who doesn't recognise you on the day starts off unsure.

                            Should it be the same as my resume?

                            The facts should match: job titles, employers and dates. The style can differ. LinkedIn is written in the first person, can cover your whole career more broadly, and has room for recommendations and media.

                            How often should I update it?

                            Whenever something changes: a new role, qualification, licence or major achievement, and a quick review every few months. Before a job search, give it a full refresh with your target job title in mind.

                                What's happening on the other side of the table

                                Whatever the job, the interviewer is trying to answer three questions about you. Meanwhile, you're answering a fourth of your own.

                                Can you do the job?

                                Skills, experience, licences and knowledge. They want proof, so give examples.

                                Will you do it well here?

                                Motivation, reliability and attitude. Why this job, and will you stick around?

                                Will you work well with us?

                                Teamwork, communication and how you treat customers and colleagues.

                                Do you want this job?

                                The interview is your chance to check the role, the manager and the workplace too.

                                Most interviews are structured now

                                A structured interview asks every candidate the same questions and scores each answer against a rating guide. Research ranks it as the best common predictor of job performance, which is why government, health, education and most large employers use it.3 For you, that means the questions are predictable and the marking rewards evidence.

                                Preparation raises your score, fairly

                                In studies of interview coaching, candidates who prepared more thoroughly received higher ratings. In one study, coaching on content and clear communication even made the interview a better predictor of later job performance.4 Preparing is how you show what you can really do.

                                The small talk counts

                                Impressions from the opening chat carry into the scores, especially for the first few questions. Those impressions are built largely from things that matter at work, like how clearly you communicate.5 The interview starts at reception.

                                Before

                                Know which interview you're walking into

                                When you're invited, ask about the format, how long it runs, who you'll meet, whether there's a test or task, and, for a trial shift, whether it's paid. Employers expect these questions, and asking them shows you're organised.

                                Phone screen Most jobs · recruiters

                                10 to 30 minutes with a recruiter or manager. Checks the basics: availability, pay expectations, right to work and genuine interest.

                                Tip: Take it somewhere quiet with the job ad and your notes in front of you. Standing up helps your voice sound energetic.

                                Live video Office roles · regional · FIFO

                                Teams, Zoom or Google Meet. It works like a face-to-face interview, with technology to manage as well.

                                Tip: Join 5 minutes early after testing the link. Put the camera at eye level and look at the lens for your key points.

                                One-way video Large employers · graduate programs

                                You record answers to set questions, usually with some thinking time and a time limit for each answer. Nobody is live on the other end.

                                Tip: Use the practice question, check whether retakes are allowed, and finish inside the limit rather than right on it.

                                AI interview High-volume roles · early rounds

                                An AI voice or chatbot asks the questions and may transcribe or score your answers before a person sees them.

                                Tip: Ask how the AI is used and whether a person reviews the result. See AI interviews.

                                One-on-one Small business · hiring managers

                                Usually with the person you'd report to. A mix of experience, behaviour and motivation questions, often more conversational.

                                Tip: It may feel like a chat, but it's still an interview. Bring your examples into the conversation.

                                Panel Government · health · education

                                Two to four interviewers taking turns with set questions, often scoring as you speak. Public service panels usually ask every candidate the same or similar questions.6

                                Tip: Answer the person who asked, then include the others with eye contact. Note-taking is normal; they're recording your evidence.

                                Group interview or assessment centre Graduates · retail · airlines

                                Several candidates at once, with group tasks, role plays, case studies or presentations. Can run half a day.

                                Tip: They're watching how you work with others. Bring quieter people in, keep an eye on time, and build on other people's ideas.

                                Trial shift or work trial Hospitality · retail · trades

                                You show your skills on the job under supervision: making coffee, serving, using tools, picking orders.

                                Tip: Ask beforehand whether it's paid and how long it runs. Most trials should be paid. See your rights.

                                Skills test, task or presentation Admin · tech · teaching · trades

                                Typing tests, written tasks, coding challenges, case studies, a sample lesson or a practical assessment.

                                Tip: Confirm the time allowed, which tools you can use (including AI) and how it's marked.

                                Psychometric tests Graduates · banks · large corporates

                                Online numerical, verbal or logical reasoning tests, and personality questionnaires.

                                Tip: Do free practice tests so the format doesn't surprise you. Answer personality questions honestly and consistently.

                                Informal chat or coffee Startups · senior roles

                                A relaxed meeting, sometimes before a formal process starts.

                                Tip: Prepare as you would for a formal interview. Relaxed settings still produce judgements.

                                Second or final round Mid-size and large employers

                                Deeper questions, more senior people, or meeting the team. Sometimes includes a task or presentation.

                                Tip: Review your notes from round one. They want new depth, not the same stories again.

                                Answer with evidence: the STAR method

                                "Tell me about a time when…" questions are the core of most structured interviews. They assume that how you handled things before is the best guide to how you'll handle them again. The Australian Public Service Commission recommends the STAR model for presenting your experience, and it works for any job.7

                                1. S

                                  Situation

                                  Set the scene in a sentence or two: where you were and what was at stake.

                                  About 15% of your answer
                                2. T

                                  Task

                                  Your responsibility or goal. What did you need to achieve?

                                  About 10%
                                3. A

                                  Action

                                  What you did, step by step. Say "I", not "we". This is what gets scored.

                                  About 55%
                                4. R

                                  Result

                                  What changed, with a number if you have one, and what you learned.

                                  About 20%

                                Aim for about 1½ to 2 minutes per answer. Six to eight good stories will cover most questions, because one story can answer several. A busy-day story can show teamwork, pressure and customer service, depending on which part you emphasise.

                                Trades

                                "Tell me about a time you spotted a safety risk."

                                Situation
                                On a house build, I noticed scaffold boards on the top level had come loose after a windy night.
                                Task
                                I was a second-year apprentice, and the crew was about to start work up there.
                                Action
                                I told my supervisor straight away instead of assuming someone else had. I kept people off that level and helped the scaffolder re-secure and re-tag it. Then I suggested adding a scaffold check to our morning pre-start.
                                Result
                                Nobody went up until it was safe, and the check stayed on the pre-start list for the rest of the job.
                                Office

                                "Tell me about a time you met a tight deadline."

                                Situation
                                Two days before month-end reporting, a system update corrupted one region's sales data.
                                Task
                                I owned the monthly report and had to get accurate figures to leadership on time.
                                Action
                                I told my manager within the hour, with a recovery plan. I pulled the raw transaction files from the backup, rebuilt the region's figures, and asked a colleague to check my totals against bank deposits.
                                Result
                                The report went out on time. I then wrote a data-check list for after system updates, which the team still uses.
                                First job

                                "Tell me about a time you took on responsibility."

                                Situation
                                In Year 11 I captained my netball team, and our coach was away for the last three weeks of the season.
                                Task
                                Someone had to run training and organise game days, and the team asked me.
                                Action
                                I set up a group chat to confirm numbers each week, ran short sessions using the coach's drills, and made a rotation so everyone got equal court time. When two players argued over positions, I spoke to each of them separately and we agreed they'd swap at half-time.
                                Result
                                We never forfeited a game, finished third, and the coach asked me to help with the juniors the next year.

                                No work experience? Use school, TAFE or uni projects, sport, volunteering, caring for family, a side business or a casual job. Interviewers are scoring the skill, not where you used it.

                                  The role

                                  • What does a typical day or week look like?
                                  • What would you want me to achieve in the first three months?
                                  • What's the hardest part of this job?

                                  Success

                                  • What separates someone who's good in this role from someone who's great?

                                  • Who would I work with most closely?

                                  Save the perks for later. Leave detailed questions about leave, bonuses and benefits until the offer stage unless they raise them. About a quarter of Australian hiring managers in one survey said asking about perks in a first interview can cost you the job.1 Basic questions about hours, shifts and location are fine at any stage.

                                  During

                                  On the day

                                  What to wear

                                  Match what people wear in the job, then go one step smarter. Clean, ironed and comfortable beats expensive. If you're unsure, ask the person who booked the interview. For a trial or practical, wear what you'd work in, with any safety gear they ask for.

                                  When to arrive

                                  Reach the building 10 to 15 minutes early and check in 5 to 10 minutes before your time. Much earlier puts pressure on the interviewer, so wait nearby. Running late? Call before the start time, apologise once and give an arrival time.

                                  The first few minutes

                                  Greet everyone by name, smile, and have an easy reply ready for "How was the trip in?" The opening chat shapes how your first answers are heard.5

                                  While you answer

                                  It's fine to pause and think, to ask for a question to be repeated, or to check "Would you like more detail on that?" Bring notes and glance at them. Answer the question asked, then stop.

                                  If your mind goes blank

                                  Repeat the question back slowly, ask for a moment, or ask to come back to it, and then do come back. A calm recovery shows the composure they want at work.

                                  Closing strongly

                                  Ask about next steps and timing, thank each person by name, and say clearly that you want the job, if you do. Plenty of candidates never say it.

                                  "I'm excited."

                                  Nervous? Tell yourself you're excited.

                                  Anxiety and excitement feel almost the same in your body: racing heart, energy, butterflies. In experiments by Harvard researcher Alison Wood Brooks, people who said "I am excited" before a stressful speech or task performed better than people who tried to calm down.8 Say it out loud in the car park, then take a few slow breaths with long exhales.

                                  words still matter

                                  The "93% body language" rule is a myth.

                                  It comes from 1960s experiments where people judged feelings from single spoken words, and it doesn't apply to communication in general.9 Your answers carry the interview. Good basics still help: eye contact, sitting up, a natural smile, and matching the interviewer's energy.

                                  Phone, video and AI interviews

                                  Phone

                                  AI interviews

                                  When the interviewer is software

                                  AI interviews are spreading fast. In a 2026 survey by hiring platform Greenhouse, 63% of US job seekers said they'd been interviewed by an AI, and 70% weren't clearly told upfront that AI would assess them.10 The survey also covered Australia, but these headline figures are for US respondents.

                                  • Ask before you start: Will AI assess my answers? Will a person review the result? Can I request a human interview or an adjustment?

                                  • Speak clearly and steadily in a quiet room, so the transcript captures what you meant.

                                  Prepare with AI. Interview without it.

                                  • Great for preparation: researching the employer, generating likely questions, running a mock interview, and getting feedback on your STAR answers.
                                  • Risky during the interview. In a 2026 US survey, 22% of job seekers admitted using AI during live interviews.11 Employers have noticed: some ban it, and some have brought back in-person rounds to check.12 Reading answers off a screen is easy to spot.

                                  Pay & rights

                                  Talking about pay

                                  Pay conversations go better when you've done your homework and you're clear on one Australian detail: superannuation.

                                  1. Research the range

                                    Check the range in the ad, salary data on job sites, and what people in similar roles earn. If the job is covered by a modern award, as many retail, hospitality, care, cleaning and trade jobs are, the award sets the minimum rate. The Fair Work Ombudsman pay calculator shows it.

                                  2. Give a range, not a single number

                                    Put the figure you'd be happy with near the bottom of it, and keep the range realistic for the role.

                                  3. Always say the basis

                                    "Plus super" means super is paid on top. "Including super" or "package" means it comes out of the figure. Employers must pay super at 12% of ordinary time earnings, so the difference is real money.14

                                  4. Time it well

                                    If they ask, answer. If pay isn't advertised, it's reasonable to ask for the range early, often in the phone screen, so nobody wastes time.16 Leave detailed negotiation for the offer.

                                  5. At the offer

                                    Ask for it in writing and take a day to consider. Check the base and package, hours, the award or agreement and classification, and the start date. Negotiate with evidence: market data, specific skills, or a genuine competing offer.

                                  Your rights in an Australian interview

                                  Federal and state anti-discrimination laws mean employers shouldn't base hiring decisions on things like your age, sex, pregnancy, relationship status, race, religion, disability, sexuality or family responsibilities. In some states, including Victoria and Queensland, even asking for that information is restricted unless it's genuinely needed for a non-discriminatory reason.17 The general rule: questions should relate to whether you can do the job.

                                  If you're asked something inappropriate

                                  You can answer if you're comfortable, steer to the real job requirement, or politely decline.

                                  "If you're asking whether I can do early starts, yes, I can be here by 6."

                                  "I'd rather keep the focus on my skills for the role."

                                  Afterwards, you can raise it with the Australian Human Rights Commission, your state or territory anti-discrimination body, or the Fair Work Ombudsman.18

                                  Ask for adjustments if you need them

                                  If you have a disability or condition that affects interviews, you can ask for changes, and the employer has to take your disability into account.19 Common adjustments include questions in advance, extra time, captioning, an Auslan interpreter, an accessible venue, a support person or a different time of day.

                                  Ask before the interview, and share only what you're comfortable with. JobAccess can advise you or the employer on 1800 464 800.

                                  Trial shifts must usually be paid

                                  An unpaid trial is only lawful if it's limited to demonstrating skills for the vacancy, it's under direct supervision, and it lasts no longer than needed, which depends on the job and could range from an hour to one shift.20 Anything beyond that must be paid at least the minimum rate.

                                  "Before I start, is the trial paid, and how long will it be?"

                                  Unsure? Call the Fair Work Infoline on 13 13 94.

                                  Referees and checks

                                  • Choose two or three recent supervisors who've seen your work up close.
                                  • Ask their permission first, then send them the job ad and the strengths you hope they'll mention.
                                  • Employers normally get your consent before contacting referees.21
                                  • Have documents ready for likely checks: right to work, a National Police Check, a Working With Children Check for roles with kids, and licences such as RSA, a White Card or a driver's licence.

                                  After

                                  After the interview

                                  1. Same day

                                    Write down the questions you were asked and how you answered. It's your best preparation for the next round.

                                  2. Within 24 hours

                                    Send a short thank-you email. In one survey, 16% of hiring managers counted skipping it as a major mistake.2

                                  3. After their date

                                    Heard nothing a day or two after the date they gave? Send one polite check-in. No date given? Wait about a week.

                                  4. If it's a no

                                    Thank them, ask for brief feedback, and ask to be considered for future roles.

                                  5. If it's a yes

                                    Get the offer in writing and check it before you resign from your current job.

                                  One follow-up, not five. Among Australian hiring managers, about one in five said failing to follow up could cost a candidate the job, and 22% said following up too aggressively could too.1

                                  MythYou can't prepare. Just be yourself.

                                  What the evidence saysCoaching studies show that preparation raises interview scores, without making the interview less accurate.4 Be yourself, prepared.

                                  Myth93% of communication is body language.

                                  What the evidence saysThat figure came from narrow experiments about expressing feelings with single words.9 What you say matters a great deal.

                                  MythInterviewers decide in the first few seconds.

                                  What the evidence saysEarly impressions do flow into scores, mostly the first few questions. They're largely based on communication skills you can practise, and the rest of the interview still counts.5

                                  MythSay your weakness is perfectionism.

                                  What the evidence saysInterviewers hear it constantly, and a scored interview rewards specifics. A real, managed weakness is more convincing.

                                  MythThank-you emails are old-fashioned.

                                  What the evidence saysHiring manager surveys keep finding that some interviewers notice when you skip one.2 It takes five minutes.

                                  MythNever be the first to mention money.

                                  What the evidence saysWhen pay isn't advertised, asking for the range early saves everyone time.16 Detailed negotiation can still wait for the offer.

                                  Frequently asked questions

                                  How early should I arrive?

                                  Get to the building 10 to 15 minutes early and check in 5 to 10 minutes before your start time. Much earlier can put pressure on the interviewer, so wait nearby.

                                  Can I bring notes?

                                  Yes. A notebook with your key examples and your questions is normal and shows you prepared. Glance at it; don't read from it.

                                  What should I bring?

                                  Two printed copies of your resume, photo ID, your notes, a pen, any licences or certificates they asked for, examples of your work if relevant, and water.

                                  How long does an interview last?

                                  Phone screens usually take 10 to 30 minutes and most interviews 30 to 60. Panels, presentations and assessment centres can run longer. Ask when you're invited.

                                  What if I don't know the answer?

                                  Say so honestly, then explain how you'd find out, or share the closest experience you do have. That beats guessing.

                                  How many rounds will there be?

                                  Commonly one to three, often starting with a phone or video screen. Ask about the process at the start so you know what to expect.

                                  How long until I hear back?

                                  Ask at the end of the interview. If they give a date, wait a day or two past it before following up. Government recruitment often takes longer than private sector hiring.7
